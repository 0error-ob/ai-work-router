# AI Work Routing: Three-Month Chinese Learning Plan

## One-line strategy

Use a strong model to design your personalized study structure and evaluate your progress, a cheap model to generate daily exercises and conversation drills, and yourself to practice speaking and decide which methods actually work for you.

## Recommended steps

### 1. Map your starting point and goals

**What to do:** Write down what "speaking Chinese" means to you—how many words, which dialect, what situations (ordering food, business meetings?), how much time daily.
**What to use:** Human judgment
**Why:** Only you know your real constraints and what "success" looks like; spending 5 minutes here prevents wasted weeks on the wrong approach.

### 2. Get a personalized 12-week structure

**What to do:** Give your answers from Step 1 to a strong model and ask it to break down your learning into phases (e.g., tones + basics weeks 1–4, conversational chunks weeks 5–8, fluency practice weeks 9–12) with weekly checkpoints.
**What to use:** A strong model planning carefully
**Why:** This requires understanding learning psychology, Chinese language structure, and how to sequence skill-building; a weak model will give you generic advice.

### 3. Build your daily exercise pipeline

**What to do:** For each week's focus, use a cheap model to generate 10–15 conversation scenarios (ordering, introductions, asking directions) with pinyin, translations, and vocabulary lists. Save these in a table.
**What to use:** A cheap/fast model executing + a structured table
**Why:** Generating exercises is repetitive and low-risk; storing them in a table makes it easy to track what you've practiced and spot gaps.

### 4. Test comprehension with checkpoint quizzes

**What to do:** Every two weeks, take a short quiz (5–10 questions) on the vocabulary and grammar you've studied. Score yourself and mark which topics need review.
**What to use:** A tool / test (a simple spreadsheet or quiz app)
**Why:** Quizzes catch confusion before it hardens into bad habits; numbers don't lie about whether you're ready to move on.

### 5. Speak weekly and record your progress

**What to do:** Every week, record yourself speaking one of the conversation scenarios aloud. Listen back and note what felt natural and what didn't.
**What to use:** Human judgment (your ear) + a simple voice memo
**Why:** Speaking is where learning actually happens; only you can feel whether you're gaining confidence or just memorizing.

### 6. Adjust mid-plan based on what's working

**What to do:** At week 6, review your checkpoint quiz scores and listen to your week-1 and week-6 voice memos. Ask a cheap model to suggest adjustments to your remaining 6 weeks (swap methods, add grammar rules, change scenarios).
**What to use:** A cheap/fast model executing
**Why:** Your initial plan won't be perfect; a quick model can remix the approach without wasting time on something broken.

### 7. Validate with a speaker or app

**What to do:** By week 10, do a 10-minute conversation with a native speaker (tutor app, language exchange partner, or AI) and get feedback on clarity and common mistakes.
**What to use:** Human judgment + a tool (conversation app or tutor)
**Why:** Real conversation is the only true test; you need outside ears to catch accent or grammar problems you've normalized.

## Check points

- **Week 2:** Can you read and repeat pinyin for 20 new words without looking at English? (Test yourself.)
- **Week 4:** Can you order food or introduce yourself in a full sentence? (Record it and listen.)
- **Week 8:** Quiz score ≥70% on vocabulary from weeks 1–8? (Use a spreadsheet to track.)
- **Week 10:** Native speaker says you're understandable in your weak areas? (Get specific feedback in writing.)
- **Week 12:** Can you hold a 5-minute conversation on a topic you didn't pre-memorize? (Unscripted conversation test.)

## When to escalate

- If checkpoint quizzes stay below 50%, ask a strong model to diagnose whether you need to slow down, switch learning methods, or focus on a specific grammar block.
- If you record yourself at week 6 and feel no progress, escalate to a tutor (human or AI) to review your study routine—something isn't working.
- If you hit a grammar or tone concept that multiple exercises haven't clarified, ask a strong model to explain it a different way.

## When to stop automation

- **Choosing a tutor or conversation partner:** You need to judge personality and teaching style; AI can suggest criteria, but the choice is yours.
- **Deciding whether to switch dialects mid-plan:** Changing from Mandarin to Cantonese, for example, is a big pivot—discuss with someone who knows your situation.
- **Interpreting feedback from a native speaker:** If feedback conflicts with your self-assessment, talk to them directly rather than asking AI to resolve it.

## Final deliverable

A **12-week study calendar** with:
- Weeks 1–12 broken into topics and milestones
- A **vocabulary + scenario spreadsheet** with 100+ examples, pinyin, and translations
- **Weekly checkpoint tests** (quiz dates and topic coverage)
- **Voice memo dates** (when to record yourself)
- A **progress tracker** showing quiz scores, dates of tutor conversations, and notes on what's working

Hand this calendar to yourself on day 1 and follow it. Update it only at weeks 6 and 10 based on checkpoints.
